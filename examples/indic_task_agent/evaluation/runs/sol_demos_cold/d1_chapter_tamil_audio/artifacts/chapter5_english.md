Chapter 5: The Journey of a Water Drop

Every drop of water on Earth is very old. The water you drank this morning may have once been part of a river in a distant land, a cloud over the ocean, or ice at the North Pole. In this chapter, we follow a single drop of water on its long journey through what scientists call the water cycle. The water cycle has no beginning and no end. It is driven by the sun's heat and gravity.

<!-- page 2 -->
5.1 Evaporation

When the sun heats up the surface of oceans, lakes and rivers, some of that water turns into an invisible gas called water vapor. This change is called evaporation. On a hot summer day, a small pond may lose several centimeters of water. Plants also release water vapor through tiny pores in their leaves; this is called transpiration. A large tree can release more than 100 liters of water in a single day.

<!-- page 3 -->
5.2 Condensation and clouds

The warm air rises with steam. As it rises, it cools down. The cooler air cannot hold as much water vapor, so the steam turns back into tiny droplets of liquid water. This is called condensation. These millions of droplets gather around dust particles to form clouds. You can see condensation in your home when water droplets appear on the outside of a cold window.

<!-- page 4 -->
5.3 Precipitation

When water droplets in a cloud combine and become too heavy, they fall to the ground as rain. In very cold places, they may fall as snow or hail. These are all forms of precipitation. In India, most rainfall occurs between June and September due to the southwest monsoon. Some places, such as Mawlynnong in Meghalaya, receive over 11,000 millimeters of rainfall in a year.

<!-- page 5 -->
5.4 Collection

Rainwater flows over the land and mixes with streams and rivers, eventually reaching the sea. Some water bodies seep into the soil and slowly flow underground. This groundwater fills wells and springs. Lakes and ponds store water for several months, which farmers rely on to grow crops during dry seasons.

<!-- page 6 -->
5.5 Why the Water Cycle is Important

The water cycle provides us with drinking water, as well as fresh water for agriculture and industry. It also shapes the weather and moves heat around the planet. Only about 3 percent of the water on Earth is freshwater, and most of that is frozen in glaciers. That's why every drop is precious. When forests are cut down, less water returns to the air, and rainfall can decrease in that area.

<!-- page 7 -->
5.6 Water Conservation

We can all help conserve water by turning off the tap while brushing our teeth, collecting rainwater from roofs in barrels, and quickly repairing leaky pipes, as a single dripping faucet wastes up to 20 liters of water per day. Use the water you use to wash vegetables for your plants. In Rajasthan, villages have been using steps and small check dams to store rainwater for hundreds of years.
