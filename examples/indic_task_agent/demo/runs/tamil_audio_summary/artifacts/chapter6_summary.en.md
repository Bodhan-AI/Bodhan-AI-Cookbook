# Chapter 6: Lines and Angles — Summary

This chapter is about the angles that are formed when lines meet. It begins by recalling basic terms: a line segment has two end points, a ray has one, and points lying on the same line are called collinear. An angle is formed by two rays starting from a common end point, called the vertex, and the rays are its arms.

Angles are classified by size: acute is between zero and ninety degrees, right is exactly ninety, obtuse is between ninety and one hundred eighty, straight is one hundred eighty, and reflex is between one hundred eighty and three hundred sixty. Two angles adding to ninety are complementary; two adding to one hundred eighty are supplementary. Adjacent angles share a vertex and one arm. When the non-common arms form a line, the pair is called a linear pair.

The chapter then states the Linear Pair Axiom in two parts: if a ray stands on a line, the two adjacent angles add to one hundred eighty degrees; and conversely, if two adjacent angles add to one hundred eighty, their non-common arms form a line. Using this, Theorem 6.1 proves that when two lines intersect, vertically opposite angles are equal. Worked examples apply these ideas, including finding all four angles from a given ratio, and showing that the angle between the bisectors of a linear pair is always ninety degrees.
