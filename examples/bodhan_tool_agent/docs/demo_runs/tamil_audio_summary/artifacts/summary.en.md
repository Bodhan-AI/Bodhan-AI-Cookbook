Summary of Chapter 3: How Plants Make Food (Class 7 Science)

Green plants produce their own food through photosynthesis. Leaves capture carbon dioxide from the air and water from the roots, while sunlight provides the energy. The green pigment chlorophyll absorbs sunlight and drives the chemical reaction that converts carbon dioxide and water into glucose (a sugar) and oxygen.

The leaf is the main site of photosynthesis. It contains many tiny pores called stomata—often more than 10,000 per leaf—that open during the day to let in carbon dioxide and close at night to conserve water.

Roots absorb water and minerals from the soil. These are transported upward through xylem tubes to the leaves. Tall trees can move water more than 100 metres high.

The overall equation for photosynthesis is:

    carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

Plants store excess glucose as starch in roots, stems, and seeds. Common starch‑rich foods include potatoes and rice.

Photosynthesis is the foundation of most food chains. Animals eat plants, and other animals eat those animals. Additionally, plants release the oxygen that humans and many other organisms need to breathe.

This chapter explains the structure of leaves, the role of stomata, the movement of water and minerals, the chemical equation of photosynthesis, and the importance of storing food as starch.
