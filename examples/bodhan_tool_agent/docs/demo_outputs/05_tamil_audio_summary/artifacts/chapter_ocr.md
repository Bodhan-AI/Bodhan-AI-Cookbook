The Water Cycle

Water on Earth is always moving. It moves from the oceans to the sky,
from the sky to the land, and from the land back to the oceans. This
continuous journey is called the water cycle.

The Sun provides the energy that drives the water cycle. Without the
heat of the Sun, water would not evaporate and clouds would not form.

1. Evaporation

When the Sun heats water in oceans, rivers and lakes, some of it turns
into water vapour. This process is called evaporation. Water vapour is
a gas, so we cannot see it.

Plants also release water vapour through tiny pores in their leaves.
This is called transpiration. A large tree can release more than 100
litres of water in a single day.

2. Condensation

As water vapour rises, the air around it becomes cooler. The vapour
cools and turns back into tiny droplets of water. This process is called
condensation.

Millions of these droplets come together to form clouds. You can see
condensation at home when drops of water form on the outside of a
cold glass.

3. Precipitation

When the droplets in a cloud join and become heavy, they fall to the
ground. Water falling from clouds is called precipitation. It can fall as
rain, snow or hail.

India receives most of its rain during the monsoon season, from June
to September. Farmers depend on this rain to grow crops such as rice.

4. Collection

Rainwater flows over the land into streams and rivers, and finally
reaches the sea. Some of it soaks into the ground and is stored as
groundwater.

Wells and handpumps draw water from this groundwater. When the
Sun heats the oceans again, the cycle begins once more.

Why the water cycle matters

The water cycle gives us fresh water for drinking, farming and industry.
It also helps to control the temperature of our planet.

We must save water and keep our rivers clean, so that the water cycle
can continue to support all living things.
